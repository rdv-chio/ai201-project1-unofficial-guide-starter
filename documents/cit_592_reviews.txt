This is my first review for a class since I commenced the program. Through this platform, I believe my voice can be heard, and I have several points to discuss regarding the course.
1. Difficulty:
• 1.1 Class Structure:
• The material isn't inherently difficult, being straightforward. However, while lecture notes provide very basic examples, intricate proofs, particularly in graph theory, are labeled as "self-paced."
• For someone with an applied math background and a job requiring daily math application, the course was uneven in difficulty. Graph theory, usually considered simple, was particularly challenging due to its presentation.
• A reliance on lemmas and words for proofs without proper guidance makes the subject harder for those accustomed to mathematical proofs.
• 1.2 Homework (HWs):
• Canvas quizzes are basic, quickly completed in under 15 minutes. In contrast, recitations only offer a few challenging problems. Yet, homework often expects students to integrate multiple concepts.
• Topics, notably in Graph Theory, are introduced in isolated Powerpoint slides without context or application, yet we're expected to integrate them seamlessly. Many of the answers to HWs in this part depended more on pure words rather than a traditional math proof.
• Real-life problem translation into math can be ambiguous, particularly challenging for non-native speakers.
• Rigorous, formal, and math heavy answers are sometimes not valued more than simplistic pure word answers.
• Even if it is said that material from each previous parts of the course are not going to appear in the subsequent parts is not true: you must master everything. There was, for example, a question in a graph theory HW that depended on a self-paced concept from part 1 of the course merged with a self-paced concept from part 3.
• 1.3 Exams:
• Exams are generally easier than HWs.
• The practice package, while useful, overemphasizes select problems. For instance, in Time Assignment 2, three out of seven problems were based on a single example from the practice package. The performance from people that, for some reason, did not read or understand that problem, was affected enormously.
• Errors, such as one in Practice Assignment 2, can lead to wasted time, affecting performance on other questions. (Even though everyone had full points back for that question).
2. Teaching Assistants (TAs) and Grading:
• TAs' Expertise:
• Most TAs are helpful; however, some lack in-depth mathematical understanding. There were instances when I was misguided, leading to incorrect HW submissions.
• At times, external sources like YouTube or other materials seemed more enlightening.
• Grading System:
• The grading rubric is opaque and rigid, not accommodating multiple valid solution methods.
• Certain conventions, like the "multiplication rule," must be explicitly mentioned, unlike standard mathematical practices.
• Grading seems more contingent on the grader's understanding than on solution validity. Detailed answers, even those spanning 5 pages, often face deductions due to minor errors without weighted consideration for thoroughness.
3. Professor Val:
• Professor Val is knowledgeable and makes sincere efforts in teaching.
• His office hours are insightful, offering significant hints and support.
• Addressing the above-highlighted concerns could elevate the effectiveness and engagement of his classes.
Conclusion: This course has valuable content but could benefit from improvements in content presentation, grading fairness, and TA guidance. Addressing these concerns can enrich the learning experience for future cohorts.

"A Disappointing Venture Into What Are Supposed To Be Fun Topics in Math"

Overall Course Rating: 3/10

TL;DR (Detailed review below):

• CIT 592 is a discrete math course covering counting & proofs, probability, and graph theory
• The lecture quality in the course is quite poor, very heavily based on proofs and theory, with little use and application of concepts, making it difficult to understand how to apply the material into homework problems (many students sought external resources to learn)
• Computer science application of material is not explained much
• The grading structure is based mostly on exams
• Practice quizzes are a joke, homeworks can be challenging and are graded extremely harshly, often with confusing wording and unclear expectations, and very stringent use of terms for the sake of "rigor"
• Exams (IN MY OPINION) were more difficult than the homeworks, and often had some small twists (synthesizing multiple different problems types) which I didn't expect or weren't encountered in practice material, making studying for them harder. Exams are also graded very harshly, and one or two small mistakes can knock you down a whole letter grade. Exam 3 is an exception, that exam is much easier than the homeworks
• TA support was abysmal, no clarification on homeworks when necessary, office hours generally not super helpful, and the head TA was often condescending and rude
• Workload is variable, some weeks are lighter than others
• The practice problems/packets for each unit are extremely difficult, and not useful for exam preparation
• Val Tannen is actually a really great professor, with fantastic office hours that provide hints towards homeworks and exam questions. His office hours are A MUST

My detailed thoughts on the course:

CIT 592 is typically the second course taken in the MCIT Program. It is the MCIT version of CIS 160 (an undergrad equivalent course also taught by Val Tannen in certain semesters), and is essentially a discrete mathematics course which one typically takes within their first or second year in an undergraduate computer science program. The goal of the course is to introduce students to mathematical ideas and concepts which are useful in computer science, with units on counting & proofs, probability, and graph theory. I'm going to be completely honest. I was really looking forward to this course, as I really enjoy math, and I think the subjects taught in this course are really fascinating. However, this course was a huge disappointment. The subjects themselves are not difficult, but they are only made artificially harder due to the way the class is run.

The class works as follows. There are three units, the first unit is on counting/combinatorics, proofs, and mathematical terminology/notation, the second unit is on probability, and the third unit is on graph theory. Each module covers lessons through a variety of lecture videos, as well as "self-paced examples" which are documents that the student has to read on their own. At the end of the module is the homework, which consists of a weekly practice quiz (with unlimited attempts), and a weekly problem set (which contains 4-5 problems to be written in Latex). At the end of each unit, there is an exam (they are called Timed Assignments in the course) covering the information in each unit. These exams are non-cumulative, and there are assigned practice problems/study guides associated with each exam.

The lecture quality of the class in my opinion, started off alright, but got increasingly worse as the units went on. I am a big fan of learning by example, especially for math. I like to learn by watching worked out examples, and different problems types with solutions worked out in class, so that we know how to tackle the homework and apply the concepts. This class takes the opposite approach, providing students heavy theory, and proving every single concept, without actually showing how to apply these concepts in a problem or real world scenario. This is a terrible way to teach math, especially for those who have little background in theoretical math (which is most students in MCIT). This meant that students were not only lost when watching lecture videos, but they actually didn't even know how to apply the concepts they learned in lecture to the homework problems. Many students had to seek out external textbooks and resources just to learn the material because they couldn't learn via the lectures. Unit 1 at least had some worked out examples because counting and proofs can be less intuitive upon first glance. However, Units 2 and 3 on probability and graph theory had hardly any worked out example problems in lecture. Only rigorous proofs regarding theory. These are mathematical concepts which can be taught at the undergrad (and sometimes even high school) level, there is no reason to explain them in a needlessly complex fashion.

Additionally, for a class titled "Mathematical Foundations of Computer Science", the lectures hardly discuss why these topics are useful in computer science, what they're actually used for, how they are incorporated in algorithms or software design, etc. Because of this, it just feels like we're learning math to learn math, without actually understanding why this is useful to know for future classes or for this program.

The grading structure of the class is something I'm not a fan of. The weekly practice quizzes account for 3% of your overall grade. The homework assignments are worth 29% of your final grade. The remaining 68% of your grade is based upon your performance on the exams. Each exam is worth 22.5% of your overall grade (67.5% for all three exams), with an honorlock practice quiz which fulfills that last 0.5% of the grade.

Practice quizzes are a joke. They can easily be done in just a few minutes, they have unlimited attempts, and there is no reason you shouldn't get a 100% in this category. The problems are too simple, and do not adequately prepare you for most homework or exam problems.

The homework assignments are more difficult and complex, and everyone talks about how hard these homeworks are. The problems are doable in my opinion at least on the surface. You see, homework assignments are tough for a few reasons. First, the lectures are inadequate at explaining the material, and the practice quizzes are too simple, so the homework is much more challenging in comparison. Second, some problems on the homework are lengthy and can take over a page (or even two) of explanation. But most of all, the grading on homework assignments (and exams) in this course is UNNECESSARILY harsh, with an extremely stringent, rigid grading rubric. You will get points taken off for not EXPLICITLY STATING EVERYTHING in a solution, even if the information was stated in the original problem. You will get points taken off for not stating obvious facts explicitly. You will get points taken off for not including certain "buzzwords" in your explanation (I'm looking at you Multiplication Rule). You will get nickel and dimed several points for these small "mistakes" even if you are 100% correct. This is due to the emphasis of "rigor" on problems in this course. I managed to get an A in the homeworks (with multiple 100s), but it didn't really feel worth it considering how much time I put in to make my explanations as "rigorous" as possible, and how little each homework assignment is actually worth in comparison to the exams which basically undo any good you do on the homework. Speaking of which...

The exams constitute the majority of the grade, and for each exam, you are permitted a double-sided notesheet. Most people say that the exams are easier than the homework. I will probably be the first to disagree with that sentiment (at least for units 1 and 2). The first two exams in my opinion were actually harder than the homework problems. Perhaps I'm not a great tester, but I personally felt that the tests are graded super harshly, and that the problems synthesized a variety of information from the lectures, homework, and practice problems, giving each problem a weird twist that I wouldn't have expected, and such, didn't know how to solve. In fact, some problems I never even recalled seeing before, because they synthesized information from multiple different problem types in a way that I didn't know how to tackle (A grader on my unit 2 exam referenced a variance formula/concept I never even saw before). And if you make some small mistakes on an exam, they're graded super harshly, so one or two SMALL mistakes can knock your grade an entire letter grade, or go from being above average to being below average. Exam 3 however, was without question, easier than the homeworks, which were far more complex than any other homework assignment in the course. This was a welcome treat for the end of the semester.

One of the worst aspects of this class was the TA support. TA support was abysmal. Many TAs try to be helpful, but still aren't the greatest at explaining concepts, or lack a complete understanding of certain concepts. Ed discussion boards were pretty much useless, because TAs hardly did anything to clarify any questions students might have had. They either gave ambiguous answers/riddles, or would lazily tell students that "the problem says what it says", or to just look at a different lecture or discussion thread. A lot of the homework problems are very poorly worded, and even a native English speaker like me has difficulty interpreting them at times. I feel bad for non-native English speakers who couldn't get any clarification on question wording, only to get points taken off due to a "wrong interpretation". Finally, some TAs, ESPECIALLY the head TA, were extremely condescending and rude in several responses to other student inquiries, sending absolutely scalding responses. This is not how you conduct yourself when trying to help other students, and I am amazed this person has been the head TA for as long as they have. TAs also handle regrade requests, because they would often make mistakes when grading homeworks, but these would take weeks to get resolved. Really inefficient TAs, as much as I hate to say it.

The professor of the course, Dr. Val Tannen, actually is a really nice, wonderful guy. Although I'm not a fan of the lecture videos (this is probably more of the fault of a math class being online, not his own doing), his office hours are extremely helpful, and have clarified things better than any of the TAs. Furthermore, he will often give hints on how to solve homework problems (essentially guiding you through 25-50% of the problem). He also hosts exam preparation office hours a few days before the exam period, and he will often give hints and narrow down specific topics to study for the exam. Dr. Tannen is actually really great, and I think every student should go to his office hours, or at the very least, watch the recordings.

The workload in this class is variable. I don't think I ever spent beyond 10 hours a week working for this class (beyond exam weeks at least), but some students spend 15-20 hours. It depends on how well you can go through the homeworks. There are also optional practice problems/packets for each exam. These questions are absurdly difficult though, and are not at all representative of the exam questions. I've found these questions to be more harmful than helpful for actually studying for the exams.

Overall, this course was a disappointment. The concepts taught in this class are interesting, but they are taught in a way that is not effective for most people to learn. TAs for the most part, were not helpful, and were often rude. Exams can be challenging, as can homeworks, and they are made even more challenging thanks to the harsh grading and rigidity of rubrics. I truly believe that much of these issues stem from the online nature of this course, and that this would have been a much better experience if the class was taught in-person. I like combinatorics, I like probability, I like graph theory, but I do not like this course.

This class was so frustrating that I’ve had to write down my grips with the class throughout the semester as a form of therapy. This is the result. See the last paragraph for some positives about the course.

My issues with this class can be broadly broken into the following: material is lazy, bad TAs, and in general the course feels like it’s out to get you.

Material is Lazy

Almost all of the lectures were recorded in 2019, which is more than three years ago as of this writing. This wouldn’t be a problem if the lectures were actually good. The videos don’t have enough explanation, it’s just the professor’s voiceover reading each word of the slide out loud. The slides read like condensed gibberish. The steps in logic are often not explained, leaving listeners confused about how one thing leads to another. There are way too many “self paced exercises” where material is typed up in a PDF for the students to learn by themselves. The issue is, these are often the more difficult parts of the material that would really benefit from someone walking through it. Instead we’re left to our own devices to decipher these exercises. Now this wouldn’t be a problem if there was a good TA support network, which leads me to the second thing.

Bad TAs

To the TAs’ credit, they do respond to questions on Ed Discussion in a timely manner. Some of the TAs very clearly make an effort to help and are very nice. But the positives stop there. Sometimes we receive a completely unnecessary condescending comment from a TA. I would like to remind TAs that the material should seem easy to you because you are a TA. The rest of us are literally here to learn said material. You’re supposed to help us do that, and being patronizing is not helping. Second, TAs are also responsible for recitations, where they walk through some example problems using class material. This can be heavily hit or miss depending on the TA. I once had a TA during recitation copy an answer from somewhere else to explain a problem to us. When asked how they got to that answer, they literally said they did not know.

Third, the TAs are awful at grading. I’m sure every student had a few grading mistakes that required regrades. With almost 300 students, 14 assignments, and 3 exams, that’s way too many grading mistakes. There are a few times where even if your answers and explanations are correct, you will be marked fully wrong if your write up does not match the exact specification. The rubric could explicitly tell TAs to accept various correct answers and TAs would still take away points if it does not match exactly to the solution they were given. I once had a grader give a counterexample to why my solution was wrong, without even checking that my solution covered their counterexample completely. Even though grading mistakes are almost always corrected upon regrade request, it is still incredibly frustrating. This happens so often that it’s hard to believe these are genuine mistakes. It really comes off as contempt for the student or TA incompetence.

Course Wants You to Fail

This course seems to be doing everything possible to trick you. The problems are intentionally worded poorly and TAs are told to resist any clarification. We’re constantly asked to write solutions to HW problems in a specific way, but are often not taught how to from the course material. For example, we’re often asked to write solutions in a closed form format, but we were never officially told what closed form format means. Looking up “closed form” online gives us different definitions than what is used in class. Eventually we’re expected to submit answers in closed form even without prompting from the problem. We then get docked points for not doing so even though that requirement wasn’t in the problem.

In the probability unit, we were told to define a probability space for every solution but were not told how to do so from lectures. The TAs got so many questions regarding this they eventually wrote up a huge guide on Ed Discussion. This guide should have existed already without students asking for it. If the class is looking for a specific way of writing a solution, we have to be told how to write it! It’s not like the class doesn’t know this. During the induction section of the course, the professor walks through clearly how to write an induction proof in lectures. But for some reason for every other topic they fail to outline in lectures how solutions should be written.

The course is intent on shoving as much math notation down our throats as possible. When something can be easily explained using plain language, the course material prefers to have everything in math notation. I remember a TA saying they “don’t understand why students have so much trouble with notation.” I’ll tell you why. It’s because we’re not mathematicians. We navigate everyday life with language that is perfectly capable of describing the concepts you’re trying to teach. We’ve had very little experience looking at math notation, how can we be expected to be able to read it accurately and easily. Math notation would be a lot easier to learn if we had a sentence in plain language that translates long chains of notation. But that would be too much work for the lazy course material.

This class requires a ton of work outside of actual learning because students have to constantly double, triple, and quadruple check answers to make sure the way they’re written fits neatly into draconian grading standards. I think I speak for a lot of students when I say that we actually appreciate a difficult class (see reviews on CIT 593), but this class simply did not give us the tools to succeed and actively tried to make us fail. We have to spend more time looking for supplementary learning material online. If we learn how to solve a problem from other sources, we have to hope that the answers we write based on outside learning will be graded fairly by TAs and accepted as correct.

More work in this class in no way means more learning, it actually detracts from our appreciation of the material. Compare this course to CIT 593 - Computer Systems. CIT 593 is known to be a lot of work, but the work serves to reinforce our learning. I would strongly recommend students not take this class and CIT 593 together because that will highlight just how bad this class is and heap on more frustration.

Positives

It’s a shame because the material in the course really is interesting. The problems are all interesting little puzzles and I feel like we’re building up a toolbox to find real solutions. Professor Tannen is extremely helpful in office hours. I often think if they just took clips from his office hours and edited it down into additional course material, this class would improve manyfold. He tries to sprinkle humor into the material and problems which does help take the stress off a little. This course can really benefit from a revamp of the teaching material and from hiring TAs who are thoughtful, good communicators, and actually interested in helping others learn.
