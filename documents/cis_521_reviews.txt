Like so many of the courses, this one is a bit hard for me to rate. From the aspect of theory and concepts, I thought that this course was amazing. The textbook is thick and dense, but quite complete. If you liked CIT5960, you'll probably love this course. If not, then you may not like it as much as the ratings would lead you to believe. From the aspect of practicality, I think that it was highly practical for my personal learning goals, but likely not practical for the average student's goals. From the aspect of quality control, the course needs a bit more of that.

For time and "difficulty": You can probably get away with an average of 10-12 hours per week and still get an A or high B in this course, if that's your thing. That puts this course slightly on the "easier" side. However, if you actually want to learn the material such that after 4 months you can confidently say that you have a firm grasp on AI, then this course is on the "harder" side. It really comes down to your attitude and how much effort you put in. I watched all of the lectures, read all of the assigned chapters (as well as some of the unassigned ones), and put a bit of extra effort into the homework. I did not attend office hours, but that was due to being busy. I would say that on average, I spent a solid 15+ hours per week. Some of those hours were spent slogging through some of the less exciting content in the book (some chapters took me nearly 4 hours to read). I would rate the course as medium-hard. Anyone who claims this course is "easy" is either A) happy with mediocrity and not trying very hard to actually learn beyond the bare minimum required to pass, or, B) overqualified for this program. I say this because many people in my cohort found the course to be far harder than its reputation implies.

The homework for the first 2/3 of the course are excellent. I felt that they did a really great job of reinforcing the concepts in the book for a variety of algorithms. The instructions were generally clear and the autograder worked pretty well. The last 1/3 of the course was NLP focused, and this homework suffered some of the problems that abound in CIS5210. I felt that the last homework, which focused on LLMs, was a wasted of time, especially since there is whole class dedicated to NLP (CIS5210). Additionally, the NLP-focused homework had a lot of bugs and typos, leading to a bit more frustration.

Quizzes were generally pretty easy, but this, I felt, sets the students up for failure on the brutal exams. Prior to our class, exams were weighted as 10%+10% of the final grade. For our class, the professor was trying to bring down the number of A's, so the exams were weighted 15%+ 15%. That would have been fine, but the exams were far harder than we anticipated they would be, mixing multiple choice and long-form constructed response. The class averages for these exams were probably around 50-60%.

Overall, I think this is a great course and a must-take if you are more interested in the computer science side (i.e. more theoretical). If you want more practical skills for SWE, this might not be the best course for that.

This is one of the most highly rated courses at MCIT, and its reputation is well-deserved. The subject matter is fascinating. The programming assignments are rewarding and help reinforce the theory taught in the lectures. Most importantly, CCB is just delightful as a professor. His lectures really bring out the excitement and the magic that is hidden in the algorithms.

If you thought artificial intelligence was just machine learning, think again. If you thought that search algorithms are just good for things like map search, prepare to have your mind blown. You will learn to cast problems that don’t look anything like search as search problems and write the code to solve them. The first half of the course should be called applied algorithms. If the core curriculum were to be expanded to 7 courses, this should be the seventh course. Below are some specifics that make this course great. Do not mistake great for easy.

1. CCB’s lectures and office hours.
2. Great assignments that will challenge you and make you a better programmer. You get unlimited submissions to the auto-grader. If you allocate enough time for each assignment (8 - 18 hours for me), you will experience the pleasure of turning the auto-grader all green.
3. Unlimited quiz submissions.
4. Two non-cumulative open note exams worth 15% each. Because nearly everyone gets a perfect or near-perfect score on 2 and 3, the exams are the only way for the professor to broaden the grade distribution. So the exams are hard and stressful, but CCB is very fair about adjusting the final grade. In our cohort the letter grade distribution was 25 A+, 74 A, 29 A-, 28 B+, 18 B, 6 B-, 3 C+, 1 C, 1 D, 1 I.
5. Extra credits that add 3 points to your final grade.

The caveat about the open note exams is that the notes have to be taken in Colab, which may be great for many things but is by far the worst note-taking platform. The reason for this is that the Ph.D. students use your Colab notes as raw material for their language models research. The only other minor qualm is that the extra credits (except the ones awarded for attending guest lectures) are of little educational value and are basically free points. Aside from these minor issues, this is really a fantastic course. So don’t stress it out. Enjoy the ride but study hard.

Here are some resources to help you succeed:

1. For the second half of the course, some students in our cohort created a question bank of over 100 exam-like questions and answers derived from the lectures and the required reading. It is available as a public Notion page at https://mcit.notion.site/CIS-5210-Question-Bank-cf290053147a4662834f4ca47ea654d3?pvs=4. Consider encouraging your cohort to do something similar for the first half.

2. The course is largely modeled after a course at UC Berkeley. You will find invaluable worksheets and solutions for many of the topics taught in 521 at https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/fa23/

3. If you want to get a head start on 521, visit its web site at https://artificial-intelligence-class.org, where you can find recorded lectures, assignments, and recitations.
